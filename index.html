About

I am a PhD candidate in Finance at Deakin University, Melbourne. My thesis, Three Essays on Corporate Adaptation to Climate Risk, Responsible Ownership, and Political Influence, has been submitted and is under examination.

My research is in empirical corporate finance. I study how firms respond to climate risk, socially responsible institutional ownership, and political frictions, with a focus on mergers and acquisitions, green innovation, and investment efficiency.

Research

Job Market Paper

Climate Change Exposure and Acquisitions of Green Assets: Evidence from the Market for Corporate Control

with Saikat S. Deb, Edward J. Podolski, and Wei Shi

Resubmission invited, Journal of Banking & Finance (September 2026)

SSRN

We study green acquisitions as a strategic corporate response to climate transition risk. Using a novel measure of green M&A constructed from targets’ patent portfolios, target business descriptions, and strategic rationales disclosed in deal filings, we find that firms facing greater climate change exposure are significantly more likely to acquire green (but not non-green) targets. The relation is stronger among firms with limited internal innovation capacity and in industries where emissions offshoring is constrained. We assess the credibility and consequences of green M&A and find that the effect is larger among firms with stronger manager-shareholder alignment, higher analyst coverage, and greater ownership by socially responsible investors. Consistent with substantive adaptation, these deals generate positive announcement returns and are followed by increased post-merger green innovation. Taken together, our results highlight green M&A as a market-based channel for corporate climate adaptation.

Socially Responsible Institutional Ownership and the Direction of Green Innovation

with Saikat S. Deb, Edward J. Podolski, and Wei Shi

We examine whether socially responsible investors (SRI) support the green transition by directing capital toward, and encouraging the development of, green innovation. Using a panel of U.S. publicly listed firms from 2002 to 2020, we find that firms with stronger records of green innovation attract greater ownership by socially responsible investors and that greater SRI ownership subsequently increases firms' green innovation output. However, both effects are concentrated in routine forms of innovation. Firms with stronger records of radical green innovation do not attract greater SRI ownership, and SRI ownership does not increase breakthrough, pioneering, or other technologically transformative forms of innovation. Instead, SRI ownership is associated with narrower inventive search and greater reliance on existing knowledge. Our findings suggest that sustainable finance encourages incremental technological progress but fails to promote the transformative innovations needed to advance the green technology frontier and support the transition to a net-zero economy.

Corporate Lobbying and Investment Efficiency

with Saikat S. Deb, Edward J. Podolski, and Wei Shi

Accepted for presentation, FMA Asia-Pacific Conference 2026

This paper examines whether corporate lobbying affects firms’ investment efficiency. While political engagement may distort capital allocation by shielding firms from market discipline, it may also alleviate frictions that discourage value-creating investment. Using firm-level lobbying expenditure data from LobbyView combined with financial data for 2000–2020, we show that lobbying is associated with significantly lower investment inefficiency. A one-standard-deviation increase in lobbying expenditures corresponds to between a 6% and 11% increase in investment efficiency. These results are robust to alternative specifications and potential endogeneity concerns. We further show that the baseline effect is driven by reductions in underinvestment and is most pronounced among firms facing greater policy uncertainty, tighter financial constraints, and greater sensitivity to government spending. Additional evidence indicates that lobbying is associated with lower effective tax burdens, reduced regulatory exposure, and greater access to government procurement. However, lobbying-intensive firms also exhibit higher carbon emissions and greater product market power. Overall, our findings suggest that lobbying can improve firms’ capital allocation while simultaneously generating broader social externalities.

Teaching

Teaching Approach

My teaching aims to make finance accessible, practical, and intellectually engaging for students from diverse academic and professional backgrounds. Beyond formulas and models, students need the intuition behind financial concepts, the ability to apply them to real problems, and the confidence to make decisions under uncertainty. When teaching equity investment and portfolio theory, for example, I begin with the investor's practical problem: comparing stocks, evaluating risk and return, and understanding the benefits of diversification.

Finance theory is most useful to students when they can see it operate in real markets, so I pair core concepts with the professional data sources used in practice. I use LSEG Refinitiv Workspace to examine price histories, company financials, peer comparisons, multiples valuation, and IPO and M&A activity, and IBISWorld industry reports to examine market size, competitive structure, cost structure, and the main drivers and risks facing an industry. Used together, these sources let students work from the industry to the firm: they assess the conditions a firm operates in before forecasting its cash flows, selecting comparable companies, or estimating its cost of capital. Working with real data also shows students where textbook assumptions hold and where they do not. They learn to test assumptions against evidence, explain the judgements behind their numbers, and present their analysis clearly, which are the skills analysts, investors, and corporate managers rely on.

Because finance practice keeps changing, I also want students to learn how to learn, so that they can adapt to new markets, data sources, and technologies. Generative AI tools are the most immediate example. Rather than avoid them, I build them into structured activities: students use an AI tool to research a set topic, take time to examine what it produced, identifying what is correct, what is missing, and what is not supported by evidence, and then bring their questions to class discussion. In one exercise, students ask an AI tool for the beta and cost of equity of an ASX-listed company and check the figures against LSEG Refinitiv Workspace. The comparison often shows that the AI answer relies on outdated prices, an unstated market index, or an unsuitable estimation window, which leads students to the questions that matter: which inputs are appropriate, and why. The aim is for students to use these tools with judgement, treating their output as a starting point to verify rather than an answer to accept.

I also use Excel-based exercises and real market examples, particularly for students less confident with quantitative material. As part of a teaching team I ran a trading game in which students use market data to make investment decisions and track portfolio performance..
